# মূল ও শাখা — ঐতিহাসিক গল্প রিসার্চ (History-Story Research)

**Status: RESEARCH DOCUMENT ONLY — not final content.** This is sourcing and fact-finding for the "ঐতিহাসিক গল্প" (history story) slot in each of the 12 pilot classes, not polished child-facing prose. Every entry here needs sign-off from an Arabic/hadith-literate reviewer (ideally someone with an *isnād*-checking background, not just general Islamic-studies knowledge) before it is turned into a story page. This mirrors the sourcing discipline of `RESEARCH_ROOT_WORDS.md` in this same folder.

Compiled: 2026-08-18.

---

## 0. How to read this document

Each of the 12 roots below gets:

1. **প্রস্তাবিত গল্প (Proposed story)** — a specific, real incident (not a general lesson or an abstract virtue) from seerah, sahaba lives, or a Quranic prophetic narrative.
2. **উৎস (Source)** — the specific book/collection, and a hadith number where I could confirm one.
3. **শিশুদের জন্য সংক্ষিপ্তসার (Child-facing summary)** — 2–3 factual sentences, no invented dialogue or embellishment beyond what is actually reported.
4. **আস্থার মাত্রা (Confidence level)** — one of three labels, used with the same discipline as "Verified" / "Not verified this session" in the companion document:
   - **উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence)** — the hadith is in Bukhari and/or Muslim (or graded ṣaḥīḥ/hasan by a recognized scholar), and I confirmed a specific reference via search this session.
   - **মাঝারি আস্থা (Medium confidence)** — the event is treated as historically solid and is repeated across standard seerah works (Ibn Ishaq/Ibn Hisham, Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum, Ibn Kathir's *Al-Bidayah wan-Nihayah*), but it comes from the seerah/maghazi genre rather than a Bukhari/Muslim isnad, or I could not pin an exact book/chapter reference this session. **Needs a scholar to verify the precise citation and exact wording before publishing as fact.**
   - **নিম্ন আস্থা (Low confidence / flagged)** — popular but has a real authenticity or sensitivity problem; I recommend against using it, or only with heavy caveats.

No hadith number below was invented. Where I was not able to confirm one this session, I say so explicitly instead of guessing. Numbering follows the sunnah.com/Bukhari numbering scheme where given, since that's the most widely cross-checkable system.

## 1. أ-م-ن (ʾ-m-n) — believe / secure / faith (ঈমান)

**Proposed story: Khadījah (RA) reassures the Prophet ﷺ after the first revelation.**

**সারমর্ম:** After the first revelation in the Cave of Ḥirāʾ, the Prophet ﷺ came home trembling and afraid, and said "Cover me! Cover me!" His wife Khadījah (RA) covered him until his fear subsided, then told him: "Never! By Allah, Allah will never disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kin, help the poor and needy, honor your guests, and help those afflicted by calamity." She then took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, a Christian scholar, who confirmed this was the same kind of revelation sent to earlier prophets.

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb Badʾ al-Waḥy (Book of the Beginning of Revelation), narrated by ʿĀʾishah (RA) — commonly cited as **Bukhari 3** (also appears in the Tafsīr section as Bukhari 4953, and in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim's Book of Faith).

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** This is one of the most famous, best-attested hadiths in the entire corpus — the canonical opening narration of both major Sahih collections.

**Note on fit:** This story is being proposed for *both* root 1 (ʾ-m-n, via Khadījah giving the Prophet ﷺ a sense of security/reassurance — "Allah will never disgrace you") and, differently, for root 5 (dh-k-r, via the Cave of Ḥirāʾ practice — see below). They draw on the same underlying hadith but foreground different scenes within it; this is a normal curriculum move but worth flagging so the two classes don't feel repetitive to a child reading both.

**Alternative (also strong, if a distinct root-1 story is wanted):** Abū Bakr (RA) earning the title *As-Ṣiddīq* ("the one who affirms truth" — from the ṣ-d-q root, not ʾ-m-n, so technically a different root, but often paired thematically with belief/trust) by immediately believing the report of Isrāʾ wal-Miʿrāj (the Night Journey) when the Quraysh mocked it, saying "If he said it, then it is true." **Medium confidence** — the core event (Abū Bakr's unwavering belief, and the "Siddiq" title) is universally repeated in seerah literature (Ibn Hishām, Ibn Saʿd's *Ṭabaqāt*), but I could not confirm this specific exchange as a Bukhari/Muslim isnaded hadith this session — treat the exact quoted wording as seerah-genre, not hadith-genre, until checked.

## 2. ع-ل-م (ʿ-l-m) — know / knowledge (ইলম)

**Proposed story: Mūsā (AS) and Al-Khiḍr — the man who journeyed to learn from someone who knew more than him.**

**সারমর্ম:** Mūsā (AS) was once asked who the most knowledgeable person on earth was, and he said "I am" — not realizing there was someone who knew things he didn't. Allah told him that at the junction of the two seas there was a servant who had knowledge Mūsā lacked, so Mūsā set out on a long journey with his companion just to learn from him, saying he wouldn't stop "until I reach the junction of the two seas, even if it takes years." When he found this man (identified in tradition as Al-Khiḍr), he humbly asked to follow him "on condition that you teach me something of the right guidance you have been taught."

**উৎস:** This is a Quranic narrative — Sūrah Al-Kahf 18:60–82. The identification of the story's background (why Mūsā set out) is preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, narrated by Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, in the Book of Prophets (**Bukhari 3400–3401**; also Bukhari 122 in the Book of Knowledge, and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2380).

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** Direct Quranic text plus an authentic hadith (agreed upon by Bukhari and Muslim) explaining its occasion. This is arguably the single strongest ʿilm-root story available — it directly dramatizes "even a prophet must humbly seek out someone who knows more."

**Note for the writer:** Keep the child-facing version to the "a great prophet still traveled far and stayed humble to learn from someone else" beat — the three specific incidents Khiḍr performs (the boat, the wall, the boy) involve a killing that is fine in the Quran's own moral logic but needs careful, age-appropriate handling if included at all; it may be simplest to reference the journey/humility framing without retelling all three episodes in a 7–10 audience book.

## 3. ر-ح-م (r-ḥ-m) — mercy (রহমত)

**Proposed story: The Prophet ﷺ kisses his grandson Al-Ḥasan, and a companion is surprised.**

**সারমর্ম:** Al-Aqraʿ ibn Ḥābis was sitting with the Prophet ﷺ when he saw the Prophet kiss his grandson Al-Ḥasan. Al-Aqraʿ said, "I have ten children and I have never kissed even one of them." The Prophet ﷺ looked at him and said, "Whoever does not show mercy [to others] will not be shown mercy."

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Adab (Book of Good Manners), narrated by Abū Hurayrah — **Bukhari 5997** (also in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2318 — *muttafaq ʿalayh*, agreed upon by both).

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** Agreed upon by Bukhari and Muslim, very short, and extremely child-relatable (a grandfather kissing a small child).

**Alternative (also excellent, and the single most commonly taught "mercy" hadith in Islamic-studies curricula):** A bedouin man urinates inside the mosque; companions rush to stop him harshly, but the Prophet ﷺ tells them to leave him and simply pour a bucket of water over the spot afterward, saying "You have been sent to make things easy, not to make them difficult." **High confidence** — narrated by Abū Hurayrah, appears in Bukhari's Book of Wudūʾ (commonly cited as **Bukhari 219–220**) and again in the Book of Good Manners (**Bukhari 6128**). I list it as the alternative rather than primary only because the specific physical detail (urination) may need more careful phrasing for a 7-year-old audience than the grandson story does — both are equally authentic.

## 4. ع-ب-د (ʿ-b-d) — worship / servant (এবাদত)

**Proposed story: Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ (RA) tortured for refusing to worship anyone but Allah.**

**সারমর্ম:** Bilāl was an enslaved man in Makkah whose owner, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, tried to force him to renounce Islam and worship the Quraysh idols instead. He had Bilāl dragged into the desert at the hottest part of the day and had a huge rock placed on his chest, but Bilāl kept repeating just one word: "*Aḥad, Aḥad*" ("[Allah is] One, One"). Abū Bakr (RA) eventually purchased Bilāl's freedom, and Bilāl later became the very first muʾadhdhin (caller to prayer) of Islam.

**উৎস:** Seerah literature — reported through Ibn Isḥāq's *Sīrat Rasūl Allāh* (preserved in Ibn Hishām's recension), and repeated in later works such as Ṣafī al-Raḥmān al-Mubārakpūrī's *Ar-Raḥīq al-Makhtūm*.

**আস্থার মাত্রা: মাঝারি আস্থা (Medium confidence).** This is one of the most universally repeated episodes in every seerah retelling and is not seriously disputed as history, but it is sourced through the seerah/maghazi genre rather than a Bukhari/Muslim isnad — an alim reviewer should confirm the exact chain before it's presented as verbatim-quoted fact (the "Aḥad, Aḥad" detail specifically). Age-appropriateness note: the persecution can be described (heat, a heavy stone, being pressured) without depicting graphic injury — the existing popular retellings for children already do this successfully.

## 5. ذ-ك-ر (dh-k-r) — remember / remembrance (জিকির)

**Proposed story: The Prophet ﷺ's practice of retreating to the Cave of Ḥirāʾ.**

**সারমর্ম:** Before he became a prophet, Muḥammad ﷺ used to love being alone to think and remember Allah. He would go to a small cave on Jabal an-Nūr (the "Mountain of Light") outside Makkah, called Ḥirāʾ, and stay there for many days at a time in quiet devotion before returning to his family — and it was during one of these retreats that the first revelation came to him.

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb Badʾ al-Waḥy, the same ʿĀʾishah (RA) narration as the root-1 entry above (**Bukhari 3**).

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence)** — same hadith as root 1, so the same sourcing strength applies. See the "Note on fit" under root 1 about the overlap.

**Alternative (a distinct sahaba story, if the overlap with root 1 is unwanted):** The Prophet ﷺ's parting advice to Muʿādh ibn Jabal (RA) when sending him to Yemen: "Fear Allah wherever you are... and let your tongue remain moist with the remembrance of Allah." **Medium confidence** — reported in Sunan al-Tirmidhī (commonly cited as **Tirmidhī 3563 / also numbered 1987 or 2678 in different print editions — I could not pin the single standard number with confidence this session**) and graded *ḥasan gharīb* by al-Tirmidhī himself, meaning it is accepted but transmitted through a narrower chain than a Bukhari/Muslim hadith — appropriate to flag as "reliably reported, slightly lower tier than Bukhari/Muslim" rather than top-tier ṣaḥīḥ.

## 6. غ-ف-ر (gh-f-r) — forgive (আস্তাগফিরুল্লাহ)

**Proposed story: The Conquest of Makkah — the Prophet ﷺ forgives the people who persecuted him for years.**

**সারমর্ম:** When the Prophet ﷺ returned to Makkah in triumph after years of being driven out, tortured, and boycotted by its people, he had the power to punish everyone who had wronged him. Instead, he gathered the Quraysh and asked, "What do you think I will do with you?" They said, "You are a noble brother, son of a noble brother." He replied, "Go — you are free," forgiving the very people who had persecuted the early Muslims.

**উৎস:** Standard seerah literature — reported across Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām's *Sīrah*, and Ibn Kathīr's *Al-Bidāyah wa-n-Nihāyah* (chapters on the conquest of Makkah); retold in *Ar-Raḥīq al-Makhtūm*.

**আস্থার মাত্রা: মাঝারি আস্থা (Medium confidence).** The overall event — a mass, near-unconditional amnesty at the conquest of Makkah — is undisputed history, agreed on across every seerah source; it is arguably *the* most famous forgiveness episode in the seerah. But like the Bilāl story, the exact quoted phrase ("go, you are free," Arabic *idhhabū fa-antumu-ṭ-ṭulaqāʾ*) comes from maghāzī/seerah narration rather than a single Bukhari/Muslim isnad, so the precise wording should be checked against a specific seerah edition before being quoted verbatim to children.

## 7. س-ل-م (s-l-m) — peace / submission (সালাম, ইসলাম)

**Proposed story: Ibrāhīm (AS) and Ismāʿīl (AS) — both submit ("aslamā") to Allah's command.**

**সারমর্ম:** Allah tested Ibrāhīm (AS) with a vision that he was to sacrifice his son Ismāʿīl. Ibrāhīm told his son about the vision and asked what he thought; Ismāʿīl answered, "Father, do what you are commanded — you will find me, if Allah wills, among those who patiently endure." The Quran says both of them "submitted" (*fa-lammā aslamā*), and just as Ibrāhīm was about to carry it out, Allah stopped him and provided a ram to sacrifice instead — the test itself, not an actual killing, was the point.

**উৎস:** Quranic narrative — Sūrah Aṣ-Ṣāffāt 37:100–107. The word *aslamā* ("[both] submitted") in verse 103 is literally the root s-l-m in its Form IV verb — the same root that gives *Islām*.

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence)** as a Quranic text — this is the single clearest place in the Quran where this root's core "submission" meaning is enacted as a story rather than just stated as a command. **Handling note, not an authenticity flag:** the Quran's own account contains no binding, no knife-at-throat description, and no violence — Allah intervenes before any sacrifice happens and it ends with a ram, not a depiction of near-death. It is age-appropriate as told in the Quran itself; the risk is only in *how* a writer might dramatize it (avoid adding invented graphic tension not in the text). One point worth flagging for the curriculum team: standard Sunni tafsīr identifies the son in this passage as Ismāʿīl (not Isḥāq/Isaac); this is the normal, non-controversial position within Sunni Islamic scholarship and not something the book needs to hedge on for a Bangladeshi madrasah audience.

**Alternative:** Salmān al-Fārisī (RA)'s long personal journey — moving from Zoroastrianism through several Christian teachers across Syria and Iraq, searching for the true religion, before finally reaching Madīnah and recognizing the Prophet ﷺ from signs he'd been told to look for — ending his search and "submitting." **Medium confidence** — narrated in Ibn Isḥāq's *Sīrah* through the chain ʿĀṣim ibn ʿUmar ibn Qatādah, and also preserved by Imam Aḥmad in his *Musnad* (no. 23737) and by Abū Nuʿaym in *Dalāʾil an-Nubuwwah*. Widely accepted as broadly reliable seerah material, but it is a long, multi-narrator account — an alim reviewer should confirm which parts of the popularly retold version are in the earliest sources versus later embellishment.

## 8. ص-ل-و (ṣ-l-w) — prayer (সালাত)

**Proposed story: Al-Isrāʾ wal-Miʿrāj — the five daily prayers are given to the Prophet ﷺ.**

**সারমর্ম:** During the Night Journey, the Prophet ﷺ was taken up through the heavens, and Allah initially commanded fifty prayers a day. On his way back down, Mūsā (AS) told him his people would not manage fifty and urged him to go back and ask for a reduction — this happened several times, each time cutting the number, until it was fixed at five prayers a day, while Allah promised the reward of fifty would still be recorded for those five.

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb Aḥādīth al-Anbiyāʾ (Book of Prophets), narrated by Anas ibn Mālik — commonly cited as **Bukhari 3342** (the fuller version of this narration also appears at Bukhari 349 and Bukhari 3887, and in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 164).

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** One of the best-known and most solidly attested hadiths in the collection, and it is *the* origin story for why Muslims pray five times a day — a very natural fit for this root/class.

## 9. ح-م-د (ḥ-m-d) — praise (আলহামদুলিল্লাহ)

**Proposed story: The two men who sneezed — only one praised Allah.**

**সারমর্ম:** Two men sneezed in front of the Prophet ﷺ. He responded to one of them — saying "may Allah have mercy on you" — but not to the other. When asked why, he explained: "This one praised Allah [said Alḥamdulillāh] when he sneezed, and the other one did not."

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Adab (Book of Good Manners), narrated by Anas ibn Mālik — **Bukhari 6221**.

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** Confirmed reference, short and clear. It is a smaller, quieter incident than the other roots' stories (no named individuals, no dramatic setting) — worth flagging that it is the weakest *narratively* (least "story-like") of the twelve, even though it is textually solid.

**Alternative (a supplementary fact, not a full narrative):** The Prophet ﷺ's own name **Aḥmad** — from the same ḥ-m-d root, meaning roughly "most praiseworthy/one who praises most" — is mentioned in the Quran as the name ʿĪsā (AS) foretold for the coming final messenger (Sūrah Aṣ-Ṣaff 61:6). **High confidence as a Quranic fact**, but it is an etymological point, not an incident/story — could work well as a short "did you know" companion note alongside the sneezing hadith rather than as a replacement for it.

**Honest flag:** I could not find a strong, well-known, *narrative* seerah/sahaba incident built specifically around the ḥ-m-d root the way the other 11 roots have one — most ḥamd material in the tradition is liturgical/doctrinal (the meaning of Al-Fātiḥah's opening, the "banner of praise" on the Day of Judgment in Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3615) rather than a concrete incident a child can follow. The sneezing hadith above is the best real, sourced incident I found, but the curriculum team should know it's a smaller-scale story than, say, the Miʿrāj or the conquest of Makkah.

## 10. ك-ب-ر (k-b-r) — greatness (اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ)

**Proposed story: ʿAbdullāh ibn Zayd's dream of the adhān.**

**সারমর্ম:** When the Prophet ﷺ and his companions were trying to decide how to call people to prayer in Madīnah, a companion named ʿAbdullāh ibn Zayd had a dream of a man teaching him the exact words of the call to prayer, beginning with "Allāhu Akbar, Allāhu Akbar" (Allah is greatest). He told the Prophet ﷺ, who confirmed it was a true vision, and asked Bilāl — who had a beautiful, carrying voice — to be the one to call it out loud for the first time.

**উৎস:** Sunan Abī Dāwūd, Kitāb aṣ-Ṣalāh, narrated by ʿAbdullāh ibn Zayd — **Abū Dāwūd 499** (graded ṣaḥīḥ by Al-Albānī; also recorded by Imam Aḥmad and Ibn Mājah).

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** Confirmed reference this session, graded authentic, and it is the origin story for the exact phrase "Allāhu Akbar" that anchors this root/class — every Muslim child already hears this phrase five times a day, so this story explains where it came from.

## 11. ق-و-م (q-w-m) — to stand / establish (ইকামত, কিয়ামত)

**Proposed story: ʿAmr ibn Salamah — the youngest boy in his tribe leads the prayer because he knew the most Quran.**

**সারমর্ম:** When ʿAmr ibn Salamah's tribe (Banū Jurmī) accepted Islam, the Prophet ﷺ told them: "Let the one among you who has memorized the most Quran lead your prayer." The tribe looked and realized that a boy of only six or seven — ʿAmr himself, who had been picking up verses from passing travelers — knew more Quran than any of the adults, so they put him forward to *stand* and lead the whole community in prayer.

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Maghāzī, narrated by ʿAmr ibn Salamah himself — **Bukhari 4302**.

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** Confirmed reference. Strong fit for a 7–10 audience specifically because the protagonist is a child their own age, and the story literally centers on someone "standing" (*yaqūmu*) to lead — a natural bridge to *iqāmat aṣ-ṣalāh* (establishing prayer), one of the core Quranic phrases built on this root.

**Alternative (broader "a people" angle on the same root — qawm also means "a people/nation"):** Nūḥ (AS) standing firm and calling his people (*qawmuhu*) to Allah for 950 years despite constant rejection, as told in Sūrah Nūḥ and Sūrah Hūd. **High confidence as Quranic text** — solid if the class wants to emphasize "qawm = a people" rather than "qāma = to stand," though it's a heavier, longer story (ends in the Flood) that needs careful pacing for this age group.

## 12. ك-ت-ب (k-t-b) — write / book (কিতাব)

**Proposed story: Zayd ibn Thābit (RA) compiles the Quran into a single written book.**

**সারমর্ম:** After the Battle of Yamāmah, many of the companions who had memorized the entire Quran by heart were killed. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (RA) grew worried that if this kept happening, parts of the Quran could be lost, and urged Abū Bakr (RA), the first caliph, to have it gathered into one written book. Abū Bakr assigned the task to Zayd ibn Thābit (RA), who had been one of the Prophet's ﷺ scribes, and Zayd carefully collected the verses — checking them against both memorized recitation and existing written pieces — into the first complete written muṣḥaf (copy) of the Quran.

**উৎস:** Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb Faḍāʾil al-Qurʾān (Book of the Virtues of the Quran), narrated by Zayd ibn Thābit — **Bukhari 4986**.

**আস্থার মাত্রা: উচ্চ আস্থা (High confidence).** Confirmed reference, and this is the single most important "book" story in Islamic history for this root — it is literally the story of how the Quran became a *kitāb* (written book) in the form Muslims hold today. Excellent fit and probably the strongest single root-to-story match in the whole set of 12.

## 14. Cross-cutting notes for the curriculum team

- **Two roots (1 and 5) currently point at the same underlying hadith** (the Bukhari 3 revelation narration), just different scenes in it. If that feels repetitive across two classes, use the offered alternatives instead (Abū Bakr/As-Ṣiddīq for root 1, or Muʿādh ibn Jabal for root 5).
- **No graphic-violence or sectarian-contested stories were used as primaries.** The one place violence is nearby (Bilāl's torture, root 4) is handled the way children's Islamic-studies material conventionally handles it — naming the pressure and hardship without describing injury in detail. The Ibrāhīm/Ismāʿīl story (root 7) contains no depicted violence at all in the Quran's own telling.
- **Three stories (roots 4, 6, and the Salmān alternative) should be treated as "medium confidence, needs isnad check"** rather than presented to children with the same certainty as the Bukhari/Muslim-sourced ones — this mirrors exactly how `RESEARCH_ROOT_WORDS.md` distinguishes "Verified" from "Not verified this session."
- All hadith numbers above followed the numbering used by sunnah.com at the time of this research (August 2026); print editions of Bukhari/Muslim/Abū Dāwūd sometimes number hadiths slightly differently between publishers, so whoever finalizes citations for print/on-page use should re-confirm against whichever specific print edition or app the madrasah reviewers will use to check them.
